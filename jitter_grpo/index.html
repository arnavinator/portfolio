    VLA / PEFT Overview

    GR00T N1.6 on PandaOmron
    GR00T N1.6 is NVIDIA's open 3.3B-parameter vision-language-action (VLA) model. Each query maps three camera images, the robot state, and a language instruction to a 16-step chunk of actions:
    • Eagle VLM backbone (1.9B, frozen): a SigLIP2 vision encoder and Qwen3-1.7B LLM (first 16 of 28 layers) embed the instruction and images as 2048-d vision-language (VL) tokens, once per query.
    • DiT action head (1.1B): 32 transformer blocks over 51 tokens (1 state + 50 action). Odd blocks self-attend; even blocks cross-attend the VL tokens, alternating text and image tokens. AdaLayerNorm conditions every block on the flow-matching timestep: it is a LayerNorm whose scale and shift are generated from the timestep embedding, $\mathrm{AdaLN}(x, \tau) = \mathrm{LayerNorm}(x) \odot (1 + \mathrm{scale}(\tau)) + \mathrm{shift}(\tau)$.
    • Flow matching: starting from noise $a^{0} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, the DiT predicts a velocity $v_\theta(a^{\tau} \mid \tau, o)$ conditioned on the flow time $\tau$ and observation $o$, and $n$ Euler steps $a^{\tau + 1/n} = a^{\tau} + \tfrac{1}{n}\, v_\theta(a^{\tau} \mid \tau, o)$ carry the flow time $\tau$ from 0 to 1 (GR00T uses $n = 4$).
    • Embodiment MLPs: the state encoder, action encoder, and action decoder keep separate weights per robot (PandaOmron is embodiment 13); inputs and outputs are zero-padded to 128 dims.
    For CoffeeServeMug, GR00T and PandaOmron exchange:
    Signal Notes
    Cameras (3 × 256×256×3) Left & right exocentric views (2 × 256×256×3); wrist egocentric view (256×256×3) Rendered at 512×512, resized to 256×256
    State (16-d) End-effector position (3-d) & quaternion (4-d) relative to the base; gripper finger positions (2-d); base position (3-d) & quaternion (4-d) No joint angles or velocities
    Language "pick the mug from under the coffee machine dispenser and place it on the counter" Fixed throughout episode
    Action (16 × 12-d) End-effector Δposition (3-d) & Δrotation (3-d, axis-angle); gripper open/close (1-d); base velocity (3-d) & torso height (1-d); arm/base mode (1-d) Operational space control (OSC) deltas, not joint angles or torques
    The policy runs at 20 Hz: PandaOmron executes the first 8 of the 16 predicted steps (0.4 s), then GR00T re-plans from new observations. Robosuite's OSC controller converts each end-effector delta into joint torques. An episode succeeds once the gripper has released the mug on the counter in the upright position.

    LoRA Targets
    Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) adapts a large pretrained model by training a small set of extra parameters while the original weights stay frozen. LoRA [Hu et al., 2021] is a PEFT method: it keeps each targeted weight $W$ frozen and learns a low-rank update, $W + \tfrac{\alpha}{r} BA$ with $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$, and $r \ll \min(d, k)$. Since $B$ starts at zero, fine-tuning begins exactly at the pretrained model. Low rank suffices because it keeps the policy close to pretrained GR00T while allowing for manageable training costs.

    Jitter-GRPO trains only LoRA adapters (rank $r = 16$, $\alpha = 32$) inside the DiT. Everything else stays frozen: the VLM backbone, the embodiment MLPs (their 3-D per-embodiment weights don't fit standard LoRA), and each block's AdaLayerNorm projection.
    • Each of the 32 blocks: attention to_q, to_k, to_v, to_out and both feed-forward projections (6 modules).
    • Output layers: proj_out_1 (scale/shift of the final AdaLayerNorm) and proj_out_2 (projection into the action decoder).
    • Total: 194 modules, 14.5M trainable parameters (0.44% of GR00T's 3.3B).

    Motivation: Investigating GR00T's Denoising

    To see how GR00T turns noise into actions, I fixed one CoffeeServeMug observation (gripper approaching the mug), held every DiT input except the noise constant, and denoised 100 noise seeds with the default 4 Euler steps. Each line is one seed's cumulative end-effector delta over the 16-step chunk.
    Three patterns stand out:
    • Similar means: averaged over seeds, 1-step and 4-step chunks follow nearly the same path (Fig. 9).
    • Extra Euler steps add spread (Figs. 7, 8):
      • Seed-to-seed variance is 1.61 with 4 steps vs. 0.025 with 1 step (summed over x, y, z): ~65× larger.
      • A second observation, taken after the grasp, shows the same gap: 1.67 vs. 0.037 (~46×).
    • One fan, not several strategies: the seeds form a single unimodal fan around the mean (Figs. 7, 10), not distinct groups of equally valid strategies.
      • The promise of flow-matching VLAs is multimodality: different noise samples should yield diverse, equally valid behaviors. The observations measured here instead suggest GR00T behaves more like a unimodal fan.

    Is the mean a good action?
    More granular denoising (more steps) leads to more spread in trajectories, but VLA still offers the mean trajectory as a valid trajectory (given the right noise draw).
    This begs the question: is the mean trajectory in fact a good trajectory such that the VLA's "unimodal fan" justified ?

    If the fan were centered on a good action, executing the chunk nearest its center should be good. To test this without touching any weights, I designed and benchmarked a custom inference-time strategy, consensus noise-mode selection: it samples several noise vectors per query, denoises them in parallel, and executes the chunk closest to their consensus.
    Denoising strategy Success, seeds 100–129 × 3 runs each
    Baseline: one noise sample, 4 Euler steps 48.9%
    Chunk closest to the mean, 1 Euler step 46.7%
    Chunk closest to the mean, 4 Euler steps 37.8%
    • Executing the consensus hurts: on seeds 100–129, success falls from 48.9% to 46.7% with 1-step candidates, and to 35.8% with 4-step candidates.
    • The fan's mean is usually not a good action, so the problem is the shape of the fan itself.
    • A better fan is either multi-modal (several valid strategies) or a tight unimodal fan around a good action.
    From Diagnosis to Jitter-GRPO
    That set the mission: reshape the fan by LoRA fine-tuning the DiT with online RL.
    • GRPO gives stable, PPO-style clipped updates. Advantages come from comparing rollouts within a group, so no value function is needed.
    • Jitter trains on neighboring noise too: each update also feeds the DiT small jitters of the sampled noise. The hypothesis is that this carves stronger basins, pulling nearby noise toward good chunks and pushing it away from bad ones, so random noise sample lands on a good action more often.

    Jitter-GRPO Theory

    Throughout, $\varepsilon$ is the exact noise the server drew for a chunk during the rollout, and $a$ is the raw output the 4-step sampler produced from that same $\varepsilon$. Jitter-GRPO fine-tunes the DiT with GRPO so that, when $a$ succeeded, slightly perturbed noise $\varepsilon'$ also leads to $a$.

    Flow Matching (FM) Surrogate Log-Probability
    GRPO's clipped objective needs the likelihood ratio $\rho = \pi_\theta(a \mid o) / \pi_{\text{ref}}(a \mid o)$, where $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the policy that collected the rollouts. An LLM reads this off its softmax, but GR00T's sampler is a deterministic ODE from $\varepsilon$ to $a$ with no tractable density. Following FPO [McAllister et al., 2025], Jitter-GRPO uses the flow-matching (FM) loss as a log-probability surrogate, evaluated along the chunk's own path at $K = 6$ flow times $\tau_k$: $$\log \pi_\theta(a \mid o) \approx -\mathrm{MSE}_\theta = -\frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \big\| v_\theta(x_{\tau_k} \mid \tau_k, o) - (a - \varepsilon) \big\|^2, \qquad x_\tau = (1 - \tau)\,\varepsilon + \tau a$$ where $\|\cdot\|^2$ averages over the $D$ valid action elements.
    • Intuition: $v_\theta$ is trained to carry noise to actions along straight ($\tau$-length) paths. If it predicts this chunk's velocity $a - \varepsilon$ well, $a$ fits what the model learned, so $a$ is likely in a high-density region of the model's implied distribution, captured by a low MSE.
    • Why MSE?: A squared error is a negative Gaussian log-likelihood, which is why $-\mathrm{MSE}$ stands in for $\log \pi$: $$p(x) \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{\|x - \mu\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \implies \ln p(x) = -\frac{\|x - \mu\|^2}{2\sigma^2} + \text{const}$$
    With the surrogate, the ratio is a difference of MSEs, $\ln \rho_i \approx \mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i} - \mathrm{MSE}_{\theta,i}$. The surrogate's unknown constant cancels, so $\rho$ measures only how the update changed $v_\theta$. Note that while $\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i}$ and $\mathrm{MSE}_{\theta,i}$ are not exact measures of log likelihoods themselves, since these constants cancel when subtracted, their subtraction is a good proxy for the ratio.

    Note that this FM surrogate fits LoRA on GR00T better than the alternatives:
    Alternative Problem on GR00T
    Exact likelihood (change of variables) ~25,600 DiT backward passes per chunk, to measure how the sampler stretches each of the 6400 noise values (50 × 128) at each of the 4 steps
    Hutchinson trace estimate ~12 DiT forward passes per chunk. Almost every chunk must first be run in reverse through the new sampler to recover its noise, and the estimate is noisy
    Denoising steps as an MDP (DDPO, DPPO) No gradient. GR00T's denoising steps are deterministic, so each step's likelihood is all-or-nothing
    Noise injected into the sampler (Flow-GRPO, ReinFlow, πRL) Changes the deployed sampler. Every denoising step gets extra random noise, which alters the executed actions and adds a noise level to tune (or a network to learn it)
    FM surrogate (used here) No problem. 6 DiT forward passes per chunk (one per $\tau_k$), under any weights; the sampler is unchanged


    Applying the FM Surrogate to GRPO on Robot Trajectories
    GRPO [Shao et al., 2024] replaces PPO's value network with a group baseline: it samples several answers to one question and scores each answer against its group. This assumes terminal rewards: an answer is graded once, and every token shares the grade. Robot rewards are not terminal, since each state sets up the rewards that follow, which is why PPO learns a value function $V(s_t)$ to score every step.
    Jitter-GRPO stays value-free by making a whole trajectory the "answer" and distributing rewards/advantage equally throughout a trajectory:
    GRPO for LLMs Jitter-GRPO
    Question A prompt One initial state (a RoboCasa kitchen)
    Answer A response, sampled $G$ times from the same question A full episode rollout (many action chunks), sampled $G$ times from the same initial state
    Reward Terminal score of the response Sparse (terminal) binary success at the end of the episode, $r \in \{0, 1\}$
    Credit The response's advantage, broadcast to every token in the response The episode's advantage, broadcast to every action chunk in the rollout
    Jitter-GRPO computes the advantage above with a fixed scale in place of the group std, $A_i = (r_i - \bar{r}_g) / 0.5$ (instead of dividing by group variance like traditional GRPO, see Advantage Normalization), and splits it evenly over the rollout's $T_i$ trained chunks, $\hat{A}_{i,t} = A_i / T_i$, so every rollout carries the same total weight regardless of length.
    The loss is the clipped objective with a per-row lower clip: $$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{i,t}\Big[-\min\big(\rho\,\hat{A},\ \mathrm{clip}(\rho,\ \rho_{\text{floor}},\ 1 + \epsilon_{\text{high}})\,\hat{A}\big)\Big], \qquad \rho = e^{\,\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref}} - \mathrm{MSE}_\theta}$$ with $\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.2$ and $\rho_{\text{floor}}$ set per row (see Relative Lower Clip).

    Only the LoRA weights in $v_\theta$ receive gradients. For an unclipped row, the loss $\ell = -\hat{A}\rho$ has gradient $\nabla_\theta \ell = \hat{A}\,\rho\,\nabla_\theta \mathrm{MSE}_\theta$:
    • Successes ($\hat{A} \gt 0$) descend their FM loss, pulling $v_\theta$ toward their chunk: reinforcement.
    • Failures ($\hat{A} \lt 0$) ascend it, pushing $v_\theta$ away from their chunk: erosion. Once $\rho \lt \rho_{\text{floor}}$, the clip zeroes the row's gradient.


    Jitter as a Jacobian Regularizer
    Success rows are trained from jittered noise, with the velocity target moved to match. At each $\tau_k$, for each $\varepsilon$, we find a variance-preserved neighboring noise whose proximity is controlled by $\lambda$: $$\varepsilon' = \sqrt{1 - \lambda^2}\,\varepsilon + \lambda\,\xi, \qquad \xi \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$ The jitter is variance-preserving, so $\varepsilon'$ is still a standard normal draw.

    What a jittered row asks. From an input $x$ at flow time $\tau$, one Euler step over the remaining time $1 - \tau$ jumps straight to the DiT's predicted clean chunk (on the chunk's own path, an exact field $v_\theta = a - \varepsilon$ lands exactly on $a$): $$\hat{a}_\theta(x) = x + (1 - \tau)\, v_\theta(x \mid \tau, o)$$ Evaluate it at the jittered input $x'_\tau = (1 - \tau)\,\varepsilon' + \tau a$, which is $x_\tau$ built from $\varepsilon'$. Substituting $x'_\tau$ and using $\tau a - a = -(1 - \tau)\,a$: $$\begin{aligned} \hat{a}_\theta(x'_\tau) - a &= (1 - \tau)\,\varepsilon' + \tau a + (1 - \tau)\, v_\theta(x'_\tau \mid \tau, o) - a \\ &= (1 - \tau)\big[v_\theta(x'_\tau \mid \tau, o) - (a - \varepsilon')\big] \end{aligned}$$ The bracket is the jittered row's FM residual against the moved target $a - \varepsilon'$, so its loss is $$\mathrm{MSE}_\theta(\varepsilon') = \big\| v_\theta(x'_\tau \mid \tau, o) - (a - \varepsilon') \big\|^2 = \frac{\|\hat{a}_\theta(x'_\tau) - a\|^2}{(1 - \tau)^2}$$ Minimizing it makes the one-step prediction from the jittered input land on $a$: from noise near $\varepsilon$, land on $a$.

    The Jacobian. The Jacobian of a map is the matrix of all its partial derivatives, with one row per output value and one column per input value, so a map from $n$ inputs to $m$ outputs has an $m \times n$ Jacobian. For the sampler that turns noise into an action, $J = \partial a / \partial \varepsilon$ is square, $6400 \times 6400$ (GR00T's raw $50 \times 128$ action tensor in and out, of which $16 \times 12$ values are real PandaOmron actions). Entry $(i, j)$ says how much action value $i$ moves when noise value $j$ is nudged, so $J$ is the sampler's local stretching factor. Its determinant, which exists only for square matrices, measures how the sampler stretches volume and sets the exact density by the change of variables: $$p(a) = \frac{p(\varepsilon)}{|\det J|} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \log p(a) = \log p(\varepsilon) - \log|\det J|$$ $p(\varepsilon)$ is a known Gaussian, but $\log|\det J|$ costs the ~25,600 backward passes ruled out in the table above.

    Jitter-GRPO instead penalizes a cheaper summary of a Jacobian $J$, its squared Frobenius norm $\|J\|_F^2$. It equals the trace of $J^\top J$, since the $j$-th diagonal entry of $J^\top J$ is column $j$ dotted with itself: $$\|J\|_F^2 = \sum_{i,j} J_{ij}^2 = \sum_j (J^\top J)_{jj} = \mathrm{tr}(J^\top J)$$ This holds for a Jacobian of any shape: for an $m \times n$ matrix $J$, $J^\top J$ is square ($n \times n$), so its trace is always defined even when $J$ itself is not square. It is the total squared stretch, or how far the output moves on average when every input value is nudged by independent unit noise. A small norm means nearby noise lands nearby (a wide basin); a large one means nearby noise scatters (a wide fan).

    How jitter penalizes the Jacobian squared Frobenius norm. Four steps:
    1. Input shift. The jitter moves the input by $x'_\tau - x_\tau = (1 - \tau)(\varepsilon' - \varepsilon) \approx (1 - \tau)\,\lambda\,\xi$, since $\sqrt{1 - \lambda^2} \approx 1$.
    2. Output shift. Differentiating $\hat{a}_\theta(x) = x + (1 - \tau)\,v_\theta(x \mid \tau, o)$ gives the one-step prediction's Jacobian, which depends on where it is evaluated: $$J(x) = \frac{\partial \hat{a}_\theta(x)}{\partial x} = I + (1 - \tau)\,\frac{\partial v_\theta(x)}{\partial x}$$ Doing a first-order Taylor expansion around $x_\tau$: $$\hat{a}_\theta(x'_\tau) \approx \hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) + J(x_\tau)\,(x'_\tau - x_\tau) = \hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) + (1 - \tau)\,\lambda\,J(x_\tau)\,\xi$$ Subtracting $a$ from both sides splits the landing error into the clean-noise error plus a jitter term: $$\hat{a}_\theta(x'_\tau) - a \approx \big[\hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) - a\big] + (1 - \tau)\,\lambda\,J(x_\tau)\,\xi$$
    3. Average over $\xi$. We now take the expected value over the $\xi$ of the squared landing error: $\mathbb{E}_\xi\big[\|\hat{a}_\theta(x'_\tau) - a\|^2\big]$.
      First square both sides of step 2, writing $\|u\|^2 = u^\top u / D$ (mean of squared components of $u$): $$\begin{aligned} \big\|\hat{a}_\theta(x'_\tau) - a\big\|^2 &\approx \big\|\hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) - a\big\|^2 \\ &\quad + \frac{2(1 - \tau)\lambda}{D}\,\big(\hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) - a\big)^\top J(x_\tau)\,\xi \\ &\quad + (1 - \tau)^2 \lambda^2\, \big\|J(x_\tau)\,\xi\big\|^2 \end{aligned}$$ Then take $\mathbb{E}_\xi[\cdot]$ of each term:
      • First term. It is evaluated at the clean input $x_\tau$ and contains no $\xi$, so averaging leaves it unchanged: $$\mathbb{E}_\xi\big[\|\hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) - a\|^2\big] = \|\hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) - a\|^2$$
      • Cross term. Everything in it except $\xi$ is evaluated at the clean input $x_\tau$, which does not depend on $\xi$. That part is a fixed vector, so it comes out of the average, leaving only the mean of the jitter: $$\mathbb{E}_\xi\Big[\big(\hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) - a\big)^\top J(x_\tau)\,\xi\Big] = \underbrace{\big(\hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) - a\big)^\top J(x_\tau)}_{\text{fixed vector, no } \xi}\ \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_\xi[\xi]}_{=\,0} = 0$$ Intuitively, $\xi$ and $-\xi$ are equally likely and flip the cross term's sign, so they cancel on average.
      • Last term. By the trace identity $\mathbb{E}[\xi^\top A\, \xi] = \mathrm{tr}(A\, \mathbb{E}[\xi\xi^\top])$ with $\mathbb{E}[\xi\xi^\top] = I$, and $\mathrm{tr}(J^\top J) = \|J\|_F^2$ from above, it averages to the squared Frobenius norm: $$\mathbb{E}_\xi\big[\|J(x_\tau)\,\xi\|^2\big] = \frac{1}{D}\,\mathbb{E}_\xi\big[\xi^\top J(x_\tau)^\top J(x_\tau)\,\xi\big] = \frac{1}{D}\,\mathrm{tr}\big(J(x_\tau)^\top J(x_\tau)\big) = \frac{\|J(x_\tau)\|_F^2}{D}$$
      So $$\mathbb{E}_\xi\big[\|\hat{a}_\theta(x'_\tau) - a\|^2\big] \approx \big\|\hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) - a\big\|^2 + (1 - \tau)^2 \lambda^2\, \frac{\|J(x_\tau)\|_F^2}{D}$$ Here $D$ is the number of action values the MSE averages over: the $16 \times 12 = 192$ real PandaOmron values ($8 \times 12 = 96$ when only the executed steps are scored, as in the runs), and $\|J(x_\tau)\|_F^2$ sums over their rows (a $D \times 6400$ block of $J(x_\tau)$).
    4. Back to the loss. Dividing by $(1 - \tau)^2$ turns the landing error back into the FM loss, and the first term is $\mathrm{MSE}_\theta(\varepsilon)$ by the same identity at $\lambda = 0$. For a well-fit chunk:
    $$\mathbb{E}_\xi\big[\mathrm{MSE}_\theta(\varepsilon')\big] \approx \underbrace{\mathrm{MSE}_\theta(\varepsilon)}_{\text{fit } a \text{ from } \varepsilon} + \frac{\lambda^2}{D}\,\underbrace{\|J(x_\tau)\|_F^2}_{\text{endpoint sensitivity}}$$
    • A Jacobian penalty for free: a success row now also penalizes how far the predicted landing point moves when the noise moves. Sampling $\xi$ estimates the Frobenius norm, as in Hutchinson's trace estimator, so no double backward pass is needed.
    • Where it acts: since $J(x_\tau) = I + (1 - \tau)\,\partial v_\theta / \partial x$, the penalty is zero when $\partial v_\theta / \partial x = -I / (1 - \tau)$, i.e. when the field fully cancels input noise. Base GR00T is already close to this in most directions, so the penalty concentrates on the few noise directions that still move the landing point: the directions that form the fan. Shrinking them widens the basin around each success.
    • Fresh $\xi$: it is redrawn on every forward pass (each epoch, minibatch, and $\tau_k$), so there is no fixed perturbation to memorize.
    Configuration:
    • jitter_pos $= \lambda$ on success rows: 0.125 to 0.4 in the experiments conducted. That jitters the noise by $\lambda^2$ = 1.6% to 16% (the share of $\varepsilon'$'s variance that is fresh noise $\xi$), leaving $\varepsilon'$ correlated with $\varepsilon$ by $\sqrt{1 - \lambda^2}$ = 99% to 92%.
    • jitter_neg $= 0$ on failure rows. A failure row raises its FM loss, so jittering it would also raise the endpoint Jacobian around the failure. That objective has no upper bound (unbounded erosion), and raising it undoes the field's noise cancellation, so noise would leak into the actions. Without jitter, failures are pushed away only at their exact $\varepsilon$.


    Optional Reinforcing Successes (AWR-on-Success)
    By FM surrogate's likelihood is biased: it can lower a chunk's likelihood freely but can barely raise it. Since $\ln \pi \approx -\mathrm{MSE}$, the ratio is a difference of MSEs, and $\mathrm{MSE}_\theta$ can only range over $[0, \infty)$: $$\ln \rho = \ln \pi_\theta - \ln \pi_{\text{ref}} = \mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref}} - \mathrm{MSE}_\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln \rho \in (-\infty,\ \mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref}}], \qquad \rho \in (0,\ e^{\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref}}}]$$ $\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref}}$ is the error of the very policy that generated the chunk. For chunks it already handles well (the ones which lead to successful trajectories and we would like to assign high advantage to), $\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref}}$ is small, so the cap is tight (often $e^{\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref}}} \approx 1.002$). This means that a success's ratio can barely rise, while a failure's can fall until the clip stops it.

    positive_advantage_weight_scaling accomodates for the bias presented in FM surrogate's inability to significantly raise a success's ratio. Only for positive advantage chunks, the Jitter-GRPO codebase allows for more heavily weighting positive advantage chunks by $k$ below: $$k = \mathrm{clip}\Big(\kappa\,\frac{M_-}{M_+},\ 1,\ k_{\max}\Big)$$ where $M_-$ and $M_+$ are the summed losses of the failure and success rows still passing gradient. So $k$ targets a reinforcement mass of $\kappa$ (positive_advantage_weight_target_ratio) times the erosion mass, capped at $k_{\max}$ (positive_advantage_weight_max).
    Since jitter rides on success rows, $k$ scales the Jacobian penalty too.
    This feature is off in the proof of concept, and included (and A/B tested) in full training ($\kappa = 1.5$, $k_{\max} = 5$).


    Relative Lower Clip
    The same difference of MSEs, $\ln \rho_i = \mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i} - \mathrm{MSE}_{\theta,i}$, shapes the clip.

    Having a uniform lower clip is uneven. On the erosion side, a flat clip stops a failure row's gradient once its ratio drops below $1 - \epsilon_{\text{low}}$. At $\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.08$: $$\rho_i \lt 0.92 \iff \mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i} - \mathrm{MSE}_{\theta,i} \lt \ln(0.92) = -0.08338 \iff \mathrm{MSE}_{\theta,i} \gt \mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i} + 0.08338$$ Every row gets the same absolute allowance. Relative to how well the row was already fit, that allowance is $$\frac{\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i} + 0.08338}{\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i}}$$ which depends heavily on $\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i}$, and $\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i}$ varies widely: in iteration 1 alone it spans 0.00032 to 0.0783, a 245× spread within one buffer.
    • 10th percentile of $\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref}}$ (a chunk the reference policy fits better than 90% of the buffer): $(0.00032 + 0.08338) / 0.00032 \approx 261.6\times$
    • Maximum (the single chunk it fits worst): $(0.0783 + 0.08338) / 0.0783 \approx 2.06\times$
    So a well-fit chunk gets ~127× more room to erode before the clip stops its gradient than a poorly-fit one. A uniform relative budget fixes this: clip_low_mse_coef $= c$ gives each row its own floor, with $\epsilon_{\text{low}}$ kept as an absolute ceiling: $$\rho_{\text{floor},i} = \exp\!\big(-\min(c \cdot \mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i},\ |\ln(1 - \epsilon_{\text{low}})|)\big)$$ A failure's MSE may then grow by at most a factor of $1 + c$. The experiments use $c = 1$ (at most double) and $\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.005$:

    Advantage Normalization
    • group_advantage_fixed_std: divide by a fixed 0.5 (the largest std of a 0/1 reward) instead of the group's own std. The group std inflates nearly-solved and nearly-failed groups: a lone failure in an 11/12 group would get −3.18, against −0.96 in a 6/12 group (−1.83 vs. −1.00 with the fixed scale). Dr. GRPO [Liu et al., 2025] drops the std term for the same difficulty bias.
    • per_iteration_advantage_norm: z-score chunk advantages once per iteration, not per minibatch. Each group's advantages sum to zero, so the iteration mean is ≈ 0 and every sign survives; a per-minibatch mean, skewed by the balanced sampler, can flip a success negative.
    • include_anchor_groups, anchor_advantage = 0.3: an all-success group has zero advantage and would be dropped by GRPO. Anchoring gives its chunks a small constant positive advantage (without jitter), so the update keeps reinforcing scenes it already solves. All-fail groups stay dropped, since pushing down every rollout gives no direction to move toward.

    n_action_steps 8 Execute 8 of the 16 predicted steps, then re-plan.
    post_reopen_keep_chunks 1–2 For episodes declared a failure, keep only 1–2 chunks after gripper reopen (if detected). The arm meanders after a failed grasp; this post-processing allows for higher signal-to-noise ratio when training.
    Minibatch construction mask_loss_with_n_action_steps on The surrogate scores only the 8 executed steps (instead of scoring all 16 steps in a chunk).
    tau_centers 0, 0.25, 0.35, 0.5, 0.6, 0.75 Flow times $\tau_k$ for the surrogate, each with a small random offset (std 0.02). Pre-training favored early $\tau$ (only 35% of its draws reach $\tau \ge 0.5$); these cover all four inference steps and put half the evaluations at $\tau \ge 0.5$, since I showed earlier how later Euler steps add the fan's spread (Figs. 7–9).
    balanced_minibatch_positive_adv_ratio, _dynamic, _max 0.5, on, 0.75–0.9 Each minibatch's success share tracks the natural share, clamped to [0.5, max]: rare successes get oversampled, rare failures do not.
    Given that we don't have an normalized probability distribution (Gaussian/softmax) we are explicitly reweighting, an isolated negative update just degrades the field locally with no guaranteed attractor. For stable training, we need a good action's path in the same minibatch to absorb the rerouted volume, such that every mini-batch has directional balance without losing data.
    mini_batch_size × gradient_accumulation_steps 8 × 16 Effective Minibatch size is 128 chunks per optimizer step (learning rate 5e-5, 3 epochs per iteration). Gradient accumulation was implemented due to insufficient VRAM size on my AWS EC2 instance (NVIDIA A10G, 24 GB VRAM).


    Related Work
    Work Idea Difference from Jitter-GRPO
    FPO [McAllister et al., 2025] FM-loss likelihood ratio inside PPO Supplies Jitter-GRPO's surrogate; no jitter
    Flow-GRPO, ReinFlow, πRL [2025] Inject noise into the sampler for exploration and per-step likelihoods Jitter-GRPO's jitter lives only in the loss; rollouts use GR00T's unmodified sampler
    ReFPO [Wang et al., 2026] Adds a reflow (CFM) loss to FPO to regularize the flow's geometry Applies to every sample and perturbs no noise; Jacobians are only measured
    [Bishop, 1995], [Hoffman et al., 2019] Training on noisy inputs ≈ a Jacobian (Tikhonov) penalty Supervised setting; Jitter-GRPO applies it to a flow policy's noise, on successes only
    I found no prior work that jitters a flow policy's input noise inside the RL objective to widen the basins of successful actions.
