    Jitter-GRPO: New Research in Fine-Tuning Flow Matching VLAs

    Jitter noise to GRPO Flow Matching surrogate for PEFT on NVIDIA's Isaac GR00T VLA

    Skills: PyTorch, RL Algorithm Design (GRPO), Flow Matching, VLA Fine-Tuning (LoRA), NVIDIA Isaac GR00T, RoboCasa Simulation, AWS EC2, TensorBoard Analysis

    Overview

    In this project, I developed Jitter-GRPO, an online reinforcement learning (RL) method for fine-tuning flow-matching vision-language-action (VLA) models. I apply it to NVIDIA's GR00T N1.6, a 3.3B-parameter VLA, on the RoboCasa CoffeeServeMug task, in which a PandaOmron robot grasps a mug from under a coffee machine and sets it upright on the counter. Jitter-GRPO builds on Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [Shao et al., 2024] and trains only LoRA adapters [Hu et al., 2021] (0.44% of the weights) on a single 24 GB A10G GPU.

    For a given observation, GR00T's noise samples form a "unimodal fan" of candidate actions, and the center of that fan is often not a good action for certain RoboCasa scenarios (see Motivation). The goal is to reshape the fan so that a randomly drawn noise sample lands on a good action more often.

    Jitter-GRPO makes two novel contributions:
    • Jitter as an implicit Jacobian penalty: on successful chunks, the policy is also trained to reach the same action from slightly perturbed noise. In expectation, this penalizes the action's sensitivity to its input noise with no extra compute or network/noise sampler change.
    • A stabilized FM surrogate for robotics GRPO: following FPO [McAllister et al., 2025], the flow-matching (FM) loss stands in for the intractable likelihood ratio, and each GRPO group compares full episodes from the same start state. AWR-on-success, a relative lower clip, and dynamically balanced minibatches correct the surrogate's success/failure imbalance.
    Jitter-GRPO improves GR00T in both experiments:
    • Proof of concept: from a hard start state where base GR00T succeeds 10% of the time, success rises to 92% within six updates as the policy learns a new approach angle.
    • Full training: across four CoffeeServeMug kitchens, success rises from 66% to 86% over 160 independent evaluation rollouts, improving every kitchen and cutting failures by 59%. Each kitchen's action fan tightens by 21–25%, and the policy passes 32% less of its initial noise directly into its actions.

    VLA / PEFT Overview

    GR00T N1.6 on PandaOmron
    GR00T N1.6 is NVIDIA's open 3.3B-parameter vision-language-action (VLA) model. Each query maps three camera images, the robot state, and a language instruction to a 16-step chunk of actions:
    • Eagle VLM backbone (1.9B, frozen): a SigLIP2 vision encoder and Qwen3-1.7B LLM (first 16 of 28 layers) embed the instruction and images as 2048-d vision-language (VL) tokens, once per query.
    • DiT action head (1.1B): 32 transformer blocks over 51 tokens (1 state + 50 action). Odd blocks self-attend; even blocks cross-attend the VL tokens, alternating text and image tokens. AdaLayerNorm conditions every block on the flow-matching timestep: it is a LayerNorm whose scale and shift are generated from the timestep embedding, $\mathrm{AdaLN}(x, \tau) = \mathrm{LayerNorm}(x) \odot (1 + \mathrm{scale}(\tau)) + \mathrm{shift}(\tau)$.
    • Flow matching: starting from noise $a^{0} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, the DiT predicts a velocity $v_\theta(a^{\tau} \mid \tau, o)$ conditioned on the flow time $\tau$ and observation $o$, and $n$ Euler steps $a^{\tau + 1/n} = a^{\tau} + \tfrac{1}{n}\, v_\theta(a^{\tau} \mid \tau, o)$ carry the flow time $\tau$ from 0 to 1 (GR00T uses $n = 4$).
    • Embodiment MLPs: the state encoder, action encoder, and action decoder keep separate weights per robot (PandaOmron is embodiment 13); inputs and outputs are zero-padded to 128 dims.
    For CoffeeServeMug, GR00T and PandaOmron exchange:
    Signal Notes
    Cameras (3 × 256×256×3) Left & right exocentric views (2 × 256×256×3); wrist egocentric view (256×256×3) Rendered at 512×512, resized to 256×256
    State (16-d) End-effector position (3-d) & quaternion (4-d) relative to the base; gripper finger positions (2-d); base position (3-d) & quaternion (4-d) No joint angles or velocities
    Language "pick the mug from under the coffee machine dispenser and place it on the counter" Fixed throughout episode
    Action (16 × 12-d) End-effector Δposition (3-d) & Δrotation (3-d, axis-angle); gripper open/close (1-d); base velocity (3-d) & torso height (1-d); arm/base mode (1-d) Operational space control (OSC) deltas, not joint angles or torques
    The policy runs at 20 Hz: PandaOmron executes the first 8 of the 16 predicted steps (0.4 s), then GR00T re-plans from new observations. Robosuite's OSC controller converts each end-effector delta into joint torques. An episode succeeds once the gripper has released the mug on the counter in the upright position.

    LoRA Targets
    Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) adapts a large pretrained model by training a small set of extra parameters while the original weights stay frozen. LoRA [Hu et al., 2021] is a PEFT method: it keeps each targeted weight $W$ frozen and learns a low-rank update, $W + \tfrac{\alpha}{r} BA$ with $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$, and $r \ll \min(d, k)$. Since $B$ starts at zero, fine-tuning begins exactly at the pretrained model. Low rank suffices because it keeps the policy close to pretrained GR00T while allowing for manageable training costs.

    Jitter-GRPO trains only LoRA adapters (rank $r = 16$, $\alpha = 32$) inside the DiT. Everything else stays frozen: the VLM backbone, the embodiment MLPs (their 3-D per-embodiment weights don't fit standard LoRA), and each block's AdaLayerNorm projection.
    • Each of the 32 blocks: attention to_q, to_k, to_v, to_out and both feed-forward projections (6 modules).
    • Output layers: proj_out_1 (scale/shift of the final AdaLayerNorm) and proj_out_2 (projection into the action decoder).
    • Total: 194 modules, 14.5M trainable parameters (0.44% of GR00T's 3.3B).

    Motivation: Investigating GR00T's Denoising

    To see how GR00T turns noise into actions, I fixed one CoffeeServeMug observation (gripper approaching the mug), held every DiT input except the noise constant, and denoised 100 noise seeds with the default 4 Euler steps. Each line is one seed's cumulative end-effector delta over the 16-step chunk.
    Three patterns stand out:
    • Similar means: averaged over seeds, 1-step and 4-step chunks follow nearly the same path (Fig. 8).
    • Extra Euler steps add spread (Figs. 6, 7):
      • Seed-to-seed variance is 1.61 with 4 steps vs. 0.025 with 1 step (summed over x, y, z): ~65× larger.
      • A second observation, taken after the grasp, shows the same gap: 1.67 vs. 0.037 (~46×).
    • One fan, not several strategies: the seeds form a single unimodal fan around the mean (Figs. 6, 9), not distinct groups of equally valid strategies.
      • The promise of flow-matching VLAs is multimodality: different noise samples should yield diverse, equally valid behaviors. The observations measured here instead suggest GR00T behaves more like a unimodal fan.

    Is the mean a good action?
    More granular denoising (more steps) leads to more spread in trajectories, but the VLA still offers the mean trajectory as a valid trajectory (given the right noise draw).
    This begs the question: is the mean trajectory in fact a good trajectory such that the VLA's "unimodal fan" is justified?

    If the fan were centered on a good action, executing the chunk nearest its center should be good. To test this without touching any weights, I designed and benchmarked a custom inference-time strategy, consensus noise-mode selection: it samples several noise vectors per query, denoises them in parallel, and executes the chunk closest to their consensus.
    Denoising strategy Success, seeds 100–129 × 3 runs each
    Baseline: one noise sample, 4 Euler steps 48.9%
    Chunk closest to the mean, 1 Euler step 46.7%
    Chunk closest to the mean, 4 Euler steps 35.8%
    • Executing the consensus hurts: on seeds 100–129, success falls from 48.9% to 46.7% with 1-step candidates, and to 35.8% with 4-step candidates.
    • The fan's mean is usually not a good action, so the problem is the shape of the fan itself.
    • A better fan is either multimodal (several valid strategies) or a tight unimodal fan around a good action.
    From Diagnosis to Jitter-GRPO
    That set the mission: reshape the fan by LoRA fine-tuning the DiT with online RL.
    • GRPO gives stable, PPO-style clipped updates. Advantages come from comparing rollouts within a group, so no value function is needed.
    • Jitter trains on neighboring noise too: each update also feeds the DiT small jitters of the sampled noise. The hypothesis is that this carves stronger basins, pulling nearby noise toward good chunks and pushing it away from bad ones, so a random noise sample lands on a good action more often.

    Jitter-GRPO Theory and Implementation

    Throughout, $\varepsilon$ is the exact noise the server drew for a chunk during the rollout, and $a$ is the raw output the 4-step sampler produced from that same $\varepsilon$. Jitter-GRPO fine-tunes the DiT with GRPO so that, when $a$ succeeded, slightly perturbed noise $\varepsilon'$ also leads to $a$.

    Flow Matching (FM) Surrogate Log-Probability
    GRPO's clipped objective needs the likelihood ratio $\rho = \pi_\theta(a \mid o) / \pi_{\text{ref}}(a \mid o)$, where $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the policy that collected the rollouts. An LLM reads this off its softmax, but GR00T's sampler is a deterministic ODE from $\varepsilon$ to $a$ with no tractable density. Following FPO [McAllister et al., 2025], Jitter-GRPO uses the flow-matching (FM) loss as a log-probability surrogate, evaluated along the chunk's own path at $K = 6$ flow times $\tau_k$: $$\log \pi_\theta(a \mid o) \approx -\mathrm{MSE}_\theta = -\frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \big\| v_\theta(x_{\tau_k} \mid \tau_k, o) - (a - \varepsilon) \big\|^2, \qquad x_\tau = (1 - \tau)\,\varepsilon + \tau a$$ where $\|\cdot\|^2$ averages over the $D$ valid action elements.
    • Intuition: $v_\theta$ is trained to carry noise to actions along straight ($\tau$-length) paths. If it predicts this chunk's velocity $a - \varepsilon$ well, $a$ fits what the model learned, so $a$ is likely in a high-density region of the model's implied distribution, captured by a low MSE.
    • Why MSE? A squared error is a negative Gaussian log-likelihood, which is why $-\mathrm{MSE}$ stands in for $\log \pi$: $$p(x) \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{\|x - \mu\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \implies \ln p(x) = -\frac{\|x - \mu\|^2}{2\sigma^2} + \text{const}$$
    With the surrogate, the ratio is a difference of MSEs, $\ln \rho_i \approx \mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i} - \mathrm{MSE}_{\theta,i}$. The surrogate's unknown constant cancels, so $\rho$ measures only how the update changed $v_\theta$. Note that while $\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i}$ and $\mathrm{MSE}_{\theta,i}$ are not exact measures of log likelihoods themselves, since these constants cancel when subtracted, their difference is a good proxy for the ratio.

    Note that this FM surrogate fits LoRA on GR00T better than the alternatives:
    Alternative Problem on GR00T
    Exact likelihood (change of variables) ~25,600 DiT backward passes per chunk, to measure how the sampler stretches each of the 6400 noise values (50 × 128) at each of the 4 steps
    Hutchinson trace estimate ~12 DiT forward passes per chunk. Almost every chunk must first be run in reverse through the new sampler to recover its noise, and the estimate is noisy
    Denoising steps as an MDP (DDPO, DPPO) No gradient. GR00T's denoising steps are deterministic, so each step's likelihood is all-or-nothing
    Noise injected into the sampler (Flow-GRPO, ReinFlow, πRL) Changes the deployed sampler. Every denoising step gets extra random noise, which alters the executed actions and adds a noise level to tune (or a network to learn it)
    FM surrogate (used here) No problem. 6 DiT forward passes per chunk (one per $\tau_k$), under any weights; the sampler is unchanged


    Applying the FM Surrogate to GRPO on Robot Trajectories
    GRPO [Shao et al., 2024] replaces PPO's value network with a group baseline: it samples several answers to one question and scores each answer against its group. This assumes terminal rewards: an answer is graded once, and every token shares the grade. Robot rewards are not terminal, since each state sets up the rewards that follow, which is why PPO learns a value function $V(s_t)$ to score every step.
    Jitter-GRPO stays value-free by making a whole trajectory the "answer" and distributing rewards/advantage equally throughout a trajectory:
    GRPO for LLMs Jitter-GRPO
    Question A prompt One initial state (a RoboCasa kitchen)
    Answer A response, sampled $G$ times from the same question A full episode rollout (many action chunks), sampled $G$ times from the same initial state
    Reward Terminal score of the response Sparse (terminal) binary success at the end of the episode, $r \in \{0, 1\}$
    Credit The response's advantage, broadcast to every token in the response The episode's advantage, broadcast to every action chunk in the rollout
    Jitter-GRPO computes the advantage above with a fixed scale in place of the group std, $A_i = (r_i - \bar{r}_g) / 0.5$ (instead of dividing by group standard deviation like traditional GRPO, see Advantage Normalization), and splits it evenly over the rollout's $T_i$ trained chunks, $\hat{A}_{i,t} = A_i / T_i$, so every rollout carries the same total weight regardless of length.
    The loss is the clipped objective with a per-row lower clip: $$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{i,t}\Big[-\min\big(\rho\,\hat{A},\ \mathrm{clip}(\rho,\ \rho_{\text{floor}},\ 1 + \epsilon_{\text{high}})\,\hat{A}\big)\Big], \qquad \rho = e^{\,\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref}} - \mathrm{MSE}_\theta}$$ with $\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.2$ and $\rho_{\text{floor}}$ set per row (see Relative Lower Clip).

    Only the LoRA weights in $v_\theta$ receive gradients. For an unclipped row, the loss $\ell = -\hat{A}\rho$ has gradient $\nabla_\theta \ell = \hat{A}\,\rho\,\nabla_\theta \mathrm{MSE}_\theta$:
    • Successes ($\hat{A} \gt 0$) descend their FM loss, pulling $v_\theta$ toward their chunk: reinforcement.
    • Failures ($\hat{A} \lt 0$) ascend it, pushing $v_\theta$ away from their chunk: erosion. Once $\rho \lt \rho_{\text{floor}}$, the clip zeroes the row's gradient.


    Jitter as a Jacobian Regularizer
    Success rows are trained from jittered noise, with the velocity target moved to match. At each $\tau_k$, for each $\varepsilon$, we find a variance-preserving neighboring noise whose proximity is controlled by $\lambda$: $$\varepsilon' = \sqrt{1 - \lambda^2}\,\varepsilon + \lambda\,\xi, \qquad \xi \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$ The jitter is variance-preserving, so $\varepsilon'$ is still a standard normal draw.

    What a jittered row asks. From an input $x$ at flow time $\tau$, one Euler step over the remaining time $1 - \tau$ jumps straight to the DiT's predicted clean chunk (on the chunk's own path, an exact field $v_\theta = a - \varepsilon$ lands exactly on $a$): $$\hat{a}_\theta(x) = x + (1 - \tau)\, v_\theta(x \mid \tau, o)$$ Evaluate it at the jittered input $x'_\tau = (1 - \tau)\,\varepsilon' + \tau a$, which is $x_\tau$ built from $\varepsilon'$. Substituting $x'_\tau$ and using $\tau a - a = -(1 - \tau)\,a$: $$\begin{aligned} \hat{a}_\theta(x'_\tau) - a &= (1 - \tau)\,\varepsilon' + \tau a + (1 - \tau)\, v_\theta(x'_\tau \mid \tau, o) - a \\ &= (1 - \tau)\big[v_\theta(x'_\tau \mid \tau, o) - (a - \varepsilon')\big] \end{aligned}$$ The bracket is the jittered row's FM residual against the moved target $a - \varepsilon'$, so its loss is $$\mathrm{MSE}_\theta(\varepsilon') = \big\| v_\theta(x'_\tau \mid \tau, o) - (a - \varepsilon') \big\|^2 = \frac{\|\hat{a}_\theta(x'_\tau) - a\|^2}{(1 - \tau)^2}$$ Minimizing it makes the one-step prediction from the jittered input land on $a$: from noise near $\varepsilon$, land on $a$.

    The Jacobian. The Jacobian of a map is the matrix of all its partial derivatives, with one row per output value and one column per input value, so a map from $n$ inputs to $m$ outputs has an $m \times n$ Jacobian. For the sampler that turns noise into an action, $J = \partial a / \partial \varepsilon$ is square, $6400 \times 6400$ (GR00T's raw $50 \times 128$ action tensor in and out, of which $16 \times 12$ values are real PandaOmron actions). Entry $(i, j)$ says how much action value $i$ moves when noise value $j$ is nudged, so $J$ is the sampler's local stretching factor. Its determinant, which exists only for square matrices, measures how the sampler stretches volume and sets the exact density by the change of variables: $$p(a) = \frac{p(\varepsilon)}{|\det J|} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \log p(a) = \log p(\varepsilon) - \log|\det J|$$ $p(\varepsilon)$ is a known Gaussian, but $\log|\det J|$ costs the ~25,600 backward passes ruled out in the table above.

    Jitter-GRPO instead penalizes a cheaper summary of a Jacobian $J$, its squared Frobenius norm $\|J\|_F^2$. It equals the trace of $J^\top J$, since the $j$-th diagonal entry of $J^\top J$ is column $j$ dotted with itself: $$\|J\|_F^2 = \sum_{i,j} J_{ij}^2 = \sum_j (J^\top J)_{jj} = \mathrm{tr}(J^\top J)$$ This holds for a Jacobian of any shape: for an $m \times n$ matrix $J$, $J^\top J$ is square ($n \times n$), so its trace is always defined even when $J$ itself is not square. It is the total squared stretch, or how far the output moves on average when every input value is nudged by independent unit noise. A small norm means nearby noise lands nearby (a wide basin); a large one means nearby noise scatters (a wide fan).

    How jitter penalizes the Jacobian's squared Frobenius norm. Four steps:
    1. Input shift. The jitter moves the input by $x'_\tau - x_\tau = (1 - \tau)(\varepsilon' - \varepsilon) \approx (1 - \tau)\,\lambda\,\xi$, since $\sqrt{1 - \lambda^2} \approx 1$.
    2. Output shift. Differentiating $\hat{a}_\theta(x) = x + (1 - \tau)\,v_\theta(x \mid \tau, o)$ gives the one-step prediction's Jacobian, which depends on where it is evaluated: $$J(x) = \frac{\partial \hat{a}_\theta(x)}{\partial x} = I + (1 - \tau)\,\frac{\partial v_\theta(x)}{\partial x}$$ Doing a first-order Taylor expansion around $x_\tau$: $$\hat{a}_\theta(x'_\tau) \approx \hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) + J(x_\tau)\,(x'_\tau - x_\tau) = \hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) + (1 - \tau)\,\lambda\,J(x_\tau)\,\xi$$ Subtracting $a$ from both sides splits the landing error into the clean-noise error plus a jitter term: $$\hat{a}_\theta(x'_\tau) - a \approx \big[\hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) - a\big] + (1 - \tau)\,\lambda\,J(x_\tau)\,\xi$$
    3. Average over $\xi$. We now take the expected value over $\xi$ of the squared landing error: $\mathbb{E}_\xi\big[\|\hat{a}_\theta(x'_\tau) - a\|^2\big]$.
      First square both sides of step 2, writing $\|u\|^2 = u^\top u / D$ (mean of squared components of $u$): $$\begin{aligned} \big\|\hat{a}_\theta(x'_\tau) - a\big\|^2 &\approx \big\|\hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) - a\big\|^2 \\ &\quad + \frac{2(1 - \tau)\lambda}{D}\,\big(\hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) - a\big)^\top J(x_\tau)\,\xi \\ &\quad + (1 - \tau)^2 \lambda^2\, \big\|J(x_\tau)\,\xi\big\|^2 \end{aligned}$$ Then take $\mathbb{E}_\xi[\cdot]$ of each term:
      • First term. It is evaluated at the clean input $x_\tau$ and contains no $\xi$, so averaging leaves it unchanged: $$\mathbb{E}_\xi\big[\|\hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) - a\|^2\big] = \|\hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) - a\|^2$$
      • Cross term. Everything in it except $\xi$ is evaluated at the clean input $x_\tau$, which does not depend on $\xi$. That part is a fixed vector, so it comes out of the average, leaving only the mean of the jitter: $$\mathbb{E}_\xi\Big[\big(\hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) - a\big)^\top J(x_\tau)\,\xi\Big] = \underbrace{\big(\hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) - a\big)^\top J(x_\tau)}_{\text{fixed vector, no } \xi}\ \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_\xi[\xi]}_{=\,0} = 0$$ Intuitively, $\xi$ and $-\xi$ are equally likely and flip the cross term's sign, so they cancel on average.
      • Last term. By the trace identity $\mathbb{E}[\xi^\top A\, \xi] = \mathrm{tr}(A\, \mathbb{E}[\xi\xi^\top])$ with $\mathbb{E}[\xi\xi^\top] = I$, and $\mathrm{tr}(J^\top J) = \|J\|_F^2$ from above, it averages to the squared Frobenius norm: $$\mathbb{E}_\xi\big[\|J(x_\tau)\,\xi\|^2\big] = \frac{1}{D}\,\mathbb{E}_\xi\big[\xi^\top J(x_\tau)^\top J(x_\tau)\,\xi\big] = \frac{1}{D}\,\mathrm{tr}\big(J(x_\tau)^\top J(x_\tau)\big) = \frac{\|J(x_\tau)\|_F^2}{D}$$
      So $$\mathbb{E}_\xi\big[\|\hat{a}_\theta(x'_\tau) - a\|^2\big] \approx \big\|\hat{a}_\theta(x_\tau) - a\big\|^2 + (1 - \tau)^2 \lambda^2\, \frac{\|J(x_\tau)\|_F^2}{D}$$ Here $D$ is the number of action values the MSE averages over: the $16 \times 12 = 192$ real PandaOmron values ($8 \times 12 = 96$ when only the executed steps are scored, as in the runs), and $\|J(x_\tau)\|_F^2$ sums over their rows (a $D \times 6400$ block of $J(x_\tau)$).
    4. Back to the loss. Dividing by $(1 - \tau)^2$ turns the landing error back into the FM loss, and the first term is $\mathrm{MSE}_\theta(\varepsilon)$ by the same identity at $\lambda = 0$. For a well-fit chunk:
    $$\mathbb{E}_\xi\big[\mathrm{MSE}_\theta(\varepsilon')\big] \approx \underbrace{\mathrm{MSE}_\theta(\varepsilon)}_{\text{fit } a \text{ from } \varepsilon} + \frac{\lambda^2}{D}\,\underbrace{\|J(x_\tau)\|_F^2}_{\text{endpoint sensitivity}}$$
    • A Jacobian penalty for free: a success row now also penalizes how far the predicted landing point moves when the noise moves. Sampling $\xi$ estimates the Frobenius norm, as in Hutchinson's trace estimator, so no double backward pass is needed.
    • Where it acts: since $J(x_\tau) = I + (1 - \tau)\,\partial v_\theta / \partial x$, the penalty is zero when $\partial v_\theta / \partial x = -I / (1 - \tau)$, i.e. when the field fully cancels input noise. Base GR00T is already close to this in most directions, so the penalty concentrates on the few noise directions that still move the landing point: the directions that form the fan. Shrinking them widens the basin around each success.
    • Fresh $\xi$: it is redrawn on every forward pass (each epoch, minibatch, and $\tau_k$), so there is no fixed perturbation to memorize.
    Configuration:
    • jitter_pos $= \lambda$ on success rows: 0.125 to 0.4 in the experiments conducted. That jitters the noise by $\lambda^2$ = 1.6% to 16% (the share of $\varepsilon'$'s variance that is fresh noise $\xi$), leaving $\varepsilon'$ correlated with $\varepsilon$ by $\sqrt{1 - \lambda^2}$ = 99% to 92%.
    • jitter_neg $= 0$ on failure rows. A failure row raises its FM loss, so jittering it would also raise the endpoint Jacobian around the failure. That objective has no upper bound (unbounded erosion), and raising it undoes the field's noise cancellation, so noise would leak into the actions. Without jitter, failures are pushed away only at their exact $\varepsilon$.


    Optional Reinforcing Successes (AWR-on-Success)
    The FM surrogate's likelihood is biased: it can lower a chunk's likelihood freely but can barely raise it. Since $\ln \pi \approx -\mathrm{MSE}$, the ratio is a difference of MSEs, and $\mathrm{MSE}_\theta$ can only range over $[0, \infty)$: $$\ln \rho = \ln \pi_\theta - \ln \pi_{\text{ref}} = \mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref}} - \mathrm{MSE}_\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln \rho \in (-\infty,\ \mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref}}], \qquad \rho \in (0,\ e^{\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref}}}]$$ $\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref}}$ is the error of the very policy that generated the chunk. For chunks it already handles well (the ones which lead to successful trajectories and we would like to assign high advantage to), $\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref}}$ is small, so the cap is tight (often $e^{\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref}}} \approx 1.002$). This means that a success's ratio can barely rise, while a failure's can fall until the clip stops it.

    positive_advantage_weight_scaling compensates for the FM surrogate's inability to significantly raise a success's ratio. Only for positive advantage chunks, the Jitter-GRPO codebase allows for more heavily weighting positive advantage chunks by $k$ below: $$k = \mathrm{clip}\Big(\kappa\,\frac{M_-}{M_+},\ 1,\ k_{\max}\Big)$$ where $M_-$ and $M_+$ are the summed losses of the failure and success rows still passing gradient. So $k$ targets a reinforcement mass of $\kappa$ (positive_advantage_weight_target_ratio) times the erosion mass, capped at $k_{\max}$ (positive_advantage_weight_max).
    Since jitter rides on success rows, $k$ scales the Jacobian penalty too.
    This feature is off in the proof of concept, and included (and A/B tested) in full training ($\kappa = 1.5$, $k_{\max} = 5$).


    Relative Lower Clip
    The same difference of MSEs, $\ln \rho_i = \mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i} - \mathrm{MSE}_{\theta,i}$, shapes the clip.

    Having a uniform lower clip is uneven. On the erosion side, a flat clip stops a failure row's gradient once its ratio drops below $1 - \epsilon_{\text{low}}$. At $\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.08$: $$\rho_i \lt 0.92 \iff \mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i} - \mathrm{MSE}_{\theta,i} \lt \ln(0.92) = -0.08338 \iff \mathrm{MSE}_{\theta,i} \gt \mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i} + 0.08338$$ Every row gets the same absolute allowance. Relative to how well the row was already fit, that allowance is $$\frac{\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i} + 0.08338}{\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i}}$$ which depends heavily on $\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i}$, and $\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i}$ varies widely: in iteration 1 alone, its 10th percentile (0.00032) and maximum (0.0783) are 245× apart within one buffer.
    • 10th percentile of $\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref}}$ (a chunk the reference policy fits better than 90% of the buffer): $(0.00032 + 0.08338) / 0.00032 \approx 261.6\times$
    • Maximum (the single chunk it fits worst): $(0.0783 + 0.08338) / 0.0783 \approx 2.06\times$
    So a well-fit chunk gets ~127× more room to erode before the clip stops its gradient than a poorly-fit one. A uniform relative budget fixes this: clip_low_mse_coef $= c$ gives each row its own floor, with $\epsilon_{\text{low}}$ kept as an absolute ceiling: $$\rho_{\text{floor},i} = \exp\!\big(-\min(c \cdot \mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref},i},\ |\ln(1 - \epsilon_{\text{low}})|)\big)$$ A failure's MSE may then grow by at most a factor of $1 + c$. The experiments use $c = 1$ (at most double) and $\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.005$:

    Advantage Normalization
    • group_advantage_fixed_std: divide by a fixed 0.5 (the largest std of a 0/1 reward) instead of the group's own std. The group std inflates nearly-solved and nearly-failed groups: a lone failure in an 11/12 group would get −3.18, against −0.96 in a 6/12 group (−1.83 vs. −1.00 with the fixed scale). Dr. GRPO [Liu et al., 2025] drops the std term for the same difficulty bias.
    • per_iteration_advantage_norm: z-score chunk advantages once per iteration, not per minibatch. Each group's advantages sum to zero, so the iteration mean is ≈ 0 and every sign survives; a per-minibatch mean, skewed by the balanced sampler, can flip a success negative.
    • include_anchor_groups, anchor_advantage = 0.3: an all-success group has zero advantage and would be dropped by GRPO. Anchoring gives its chunks a small constant positive advantage (without jitter), so the update keeps reinforcing scenes it already solves. All-fail groups stay dropped, since pushing down every rollout gives no direction to move toward.

    Related Work
    Jitter-GRPO starts from FPO's FM surrogate [McAllister et al., 2025] and adds three wins:
    • A Jacobian regularizer that falls out of jitter. Jittering a success's input noise adds an endpoint-Jacobian penalty in expectation, which narrows the fan around each successful chunk at no extra compute: no double backward pass, no extra network, and no sampler change.
    • A stabilized FM surrogate. The surrogate is lopsided: since $\mathrm{MSE}_\theta \ge 0$, a success's ratio can rise only to $e^{\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref}}} \approx 1.002$, a failure's can fall freely, and without a normalized distribution an isolated push on a failure only degrades the field. Jitter-GRPO counters all three: AWR-on-success up-weights successes to make up for their small ratio gain, the relative lower clip budgets erosion by each chunk's own fit, and dynamic balanced minibatches pair every erosion step with successes to absorb it.
    • Online RL for a 3.3B flow-matching VLA on one GPU. Whole episodes act as GRPO answers with value-free credit, failure tails are trimmed at the gripper's reopen, and LoRA trains 0.44% of GR00T's weights on a single 24 GB A10G.
    Side by side with the closest prior work, only Jitter-GRPO checks every column:

    Proof of Concept Example (overfit on single scene)

    For this project, I chose the RoboCasa scene CoffeeServeMug, in which the GR00T VLA commands the PandaOmron embodiment to approach a coffee mug underneath the coffee machine, grasp it carefully, and place the mug in the upright position on the countertop (thereby serving the coffee). NVIDIA reports that the base model (GR00T N1.6) can successfully complete this task 63.5% of the time (measured across 200 samples).

    Before training across many kitchens simulating CoffeeServeMug, the first test is whether Jitter-GRPO can learn at all: overfit one hard state and raise its success rate.
    To do so, I found and saved a state of CoffeeServeMug in which the GR00T model particularly struggles.
    Every rollout in this section starts from the same saved simulator state, step 10 of a base GR00T CoffeeServeMug episode.

    From this state, measured over 40 independent rollouts, the base GR00T model has a ~10% chance of successfully completing this CoffeeServeMug scene. We want to see if the Jitter-GRPO framework can raise the success rate.

    • Step 10, the start state: base GR00T is approaching the mug from a risky angle, with its gripper coming in close to the coffee machine. The wrist camera still sees the mug.
    • Step 12, two chunks later: base GR00T positions itself such that most of the wrist camera's view is blocked, making grasping/approach much harder.
    To succeed, the policy must adjust its approach, then grasp and release the mug despite the obscured view, instead of drifting into states like step 12.

    Training: 10% → 92% Success
    Each iteration rolls out 4 groups of 12 episodes from the start state (all 48 episodes have the same starting point, but are still partitioned into 4 groups for the sake of GRPO's objective... this is okay and adds some "regularization" in advantage calculations).
    Note that this section tests the jitter mechanism with no confounding, as the optional AWR-on-success is disabled.
    The plot below shows the success rate of the 48 on-policy rollouts that occur at the start of each iteration. Since on-policy data is collected at the start of each iteration, the success rate reported for iteration $N$ is the result of the model training in iteration $N-1$.
    • Nearly 9× the base success rate: success climbs from 10% to 92% in six updates, then holds at 81–92%. From iteration 6 on, even the weakest group of 12 succeeds at least 75% of the time.
    • More headroom (after iteration 4): the first run, at jitter_pos = 0.125, plateaued, so I resumed from its iteration-3 checkpoint at jitter_pos = 0.25. Four times the jitter variance (1.6% → 6.25%) gives success rows more room to raise their ratio, and the Jacobian term's gradient grows from ~15% to ~33% of the reinforcement gradient. (The iteration-4 dip is sampling noise: the same checkpoint scored 79% when first sampled.)
    • Stop oversampling failures (after iteration 8): near 90% success, the balanced sampler's 0.75 cap on each minibatch's success share kept oversampling the few failures. Raising the cap to 0.9 lets minibatches follow the natural share, and training stays stable.

    Learning a New Approach Angle
    To see what changed, I repeated the motivation's 100-seed sweep through base GR00T and the iteration-8 checkpoint (92% success at iteration 9), with the same 100 noise seeds for both: first from the start state (step 10), then from the state base GR00T reached two chunks later (step 12).

    • Same reach, new direction: x is unchanged (5.06 → 5.07) and z dips slightly (−3.07 → −3.16), but y changes shape. Base levels off near 1.4, while iteration 8 keeps sweeping to 1.95 (+34%). By the end of the 8 executed steps, the two y fans no longer overlap.
    • A rotated approach: the chunk's heading in the horizontal plane turns from 16° to 21°, and it turns for all 100 noise seeds. The whole fan moved, so a random noise sample now lands on the new approach.
    • Just the first chunk: this is one state and one chunk. Much of the gain may come from later in the episode (the grasp and the release), which these sweeps don't show.

    Step 12: Reacting to a Blocked View
    The same comparison at step 12 is shown (the camera view is in Fig. 11, bottom row). Note that there is no guarantee that the new policy actually ends up in this state, so its behavior in this state is purely for analysis:
    • Every seed shifts the same way: iteration 8 reaches less far forward (x: 3.06 → 2.85), swings further sideways (y: 1.44 → 1.82), and drops lower (z: −0.83 → −1.00), turning its heading from 25° to 33°. All 100 seeds shift this way in x and y.

    Full Training Example

    With the proof of concept passed on a difficult state, full training scales Jitter-GRPO to four random, unique CoffeeServeMug kitchens (a frozen scene pool, seeds 100067–103067). Each group corresponds to one kitchen seed, and each group runs 12 episodes from the first frame of that kitchen, for a total of 48 RoboCasa episodes per iteration of on-policy rollout data collection.
    Across these four kitchens, base GR00T succeeds 66% of the time (verified via 160 independent rollouts of the base policy).

    Training and Evaluation: 66% → 86% Across Four Kitchens
    Training: Enough Jitter, Then AWR-on-Success
    • λ = 0.25 is too little jitter for four diverse kitchens: a first run at λ = 0.25, enough for the single state, stayed flat near 65% and then slipped. The FM surrogate caps a success's ratio near $e^{\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ref}}} \approx 1.002$, so with too little jitter, success rows have almost no headroom and erosion wins. No jitter at all would leave even less.
    • λ = 0.4 learns: 16% jitter variance lifts success from 65% to 81% in four updates.
    • AWR-on-success holds the lead: without it, the λ = 0.4 run peaked at 90% and then slid back to 73–75%. With AWR-on-success switched on after iteration 6 ($\kappa = 1.5$), reinforcement carries 1.6–1.8× the erosion mass, and success holds at 77–90%, ending at 85%. (The iteration-6 dip is sampling noise: the same checkpoint scored 90% in the run without AWR-on-success.)

    Independent Evaluation
    To confirm the training curve, I evaluated the iteration-9 checkpoint (the policy behind iteration 10) with 40 fresh rollouts per kitchen, against the same evaluation of base GR00T:
    • Every kitchen improves: +12.5 to +27.5 points per kitchen, and 66.3% → 86.3% overall, matching the 85% training curve.
    • Failures drop by 59%: from 54 of 160 rollouts to 22. The hardest kitchen (102067) climbs from 52.5% to 72.5%, and 101067 reaches 97.5%.
    • Successes come faster: a successful episode takes 333 steps on average, against 360 for base GR00T (−8%).
    • Gains transfer to unseen kitchens: on 30 held-out kitchens (seeds 100–129, none of which were in the training set), success rose from 48.9% to 60.0% (+11 points). Part of what the policy learned in four kitchens carries over to 30 kitchens it has never seen, suggesting a future direction of including even more kitchens in the training set.

    Jacobian Penalty Leads to Less Noise Leaking into the Actions
    As a result of Jitter-GRPO's Jacobian penalty, less noise leaks into the actions. All 100 seeds in a kitchen use the same observation and the same weights, so any difference between their chunks comes from the noise. For each seed $s$, take the DiT's raw 16×3 end-effector action $a_s$ and its initial noise $\varepsilon_s$. Both live in the same normalized space, because denoising starts at $a^0 = \varepsilon$.
    1. Keep only the high frequencies.
      • Subtract the seed-average chunk, which is identical for every seed and contains no noise.
      • Take an FFT (Fourier transform) of each 16-step sequence and zero the three slowest frequencies: the constant offset, then 1 and 2 cycles per chunk. This is $\tilde{a}$.
      • $\tilde{\varepsilon}$ is the noise put through the same two steps: subtract the seed-average noise (nearly zero, since each seed's noise is random) and zero its three slowest frequencies.
      • Why drop the three slowest modes?
        • They are where the noise is supposed to act: a seed picks the chunk's overall direction and curvature within the fan, which is exactly what Figs. 17–19 measure. That's choosing a motion, not copying noise, so leaving them in would just re-measure fan width. The noise, in contrast, is white, with equal energy at every frequency, while real motions are smooth. Fast content that tracks the noise step for step is therefore leakage, and it shows up as jerk.
        • Across the four kitchens, 97–98.5% of the actions' seed-to-seed energy sits in those three modes, against about 30% for the white noise.
    2. Regress: fit $\tilde{a} \approx \beta\,\tilde{\varepsilon}$ by least squares over every seed, step and axis, $$\beta = \frac{\sum \tilde{a} \cdot \tilde{\varepsilon}}{\sum \tilde{\varepsilon}^{\,2}}$$ Plot each noise value against the matching action value, and $\beta$ is the slope of the best-fit line. There's no intercept because both are zero-mean.
      • Why is the slope the share passed through unchanged?
        • Suppose the action's fast part is a scaled copy of the noise's fast part plus something unrelated, $\tilde{a} = c\,\tilde{\varepsilon} + r$. Then the fit recovers $\beta \approx c$, because the unrelated part $r$ averages out. "Unchanged" means the same step-by-step pattern, just scaled by $c$.
        • At $\tau = 0$ the chunk is the noise, so $c = 1$; a perfect denoiser would erase it, so $c = 0$. Base GR00T keeps about 0.5–0.7%.
    Fig. 20 reports $\beta$ for base GR00T and the checkpoint in each kitchen:
    In every kitchen, the checkpoint passes 17–43% less of its initial noise straight into its actions (0.68× base on average).

    Isn't this just the tighter fan? If the checkpoint only shrank every seed's deviation from the mean, the fast wiggle would shrink by the same factor as the fan and still copy the noise exactly. Two checks say otherwise:
    • The fast wiggle shrinks much less than the fan: to 0.84–0.94× base, against 0.77–0.79× for the three slow modes.
    • What's left looks less like the noise. The correlation between each seed's $\tilde{a}$ and its own $\tilde{\varepsilon}$, which no amount of shrinking can change, falls from 0.41–0.44 to 0.26–0.31.
    The drop in noise passed through comes mostly from the wiggle decorrelating from the noise, not from the fan getting smaller.

    This is exactly the behavior the Jacobian penalty was built to produce. On every success, a jittered row asks the DiT to land on the same action from nearby noise, which in expectation penalizes the endpoint Jacobian $\|I + (1 - \tau)\,\partial v_\theta / \partial x\|_F^2$: how far the predicted action moves when the noise moves. A field with a smaller endpoint Jacobian absorbs input noise instead of copying it into the action, and that is what the measurement shows, in all four kitchens, with no extra network, no double backward pass, and no change to the sampler. Together with the 21–25% tighter fans above, both ends of the spectrum respond: the slow, noise-driven choice of motion narrows, and the fast wiggle copied from the noise shrinks.

    Full Training PASSED: with jitter, along with late-enabled AWR-on-success for stability, Jitter-GRPO raised GR00T's success across four kitchens from 66% to 86%, cut failures by 59%, and tightened every fan.


    Future Directions / Reflections

    Reflections
    • Larger groups are the clearest next lever. GRPO learns only from groups that mix successes and failures. At 85–90% success, a group of 12 rollouts often comes back all-success: in the last full-training iterations, one of the four kitchens did so in 5 of 6 iterations, leaving a quarter of each batch without an improvement signal. Doubling the group to 24 cuts the odds of an all-success group at 90% success from 28% to 8%, samples twice as many of the rare failures and successes, and tightens each kitchen's baseline from ±10 to ±7 points (at 85% success). The catch is cost: collection time grows linearly with group size, and I'm GPU poor, so every extra rollout is more AWS EC2 time.
    • On-policy data is expensive. Simulating one episode takes about 2.4 minutes of wall-clock time (physics, three cameras, and ~45 GR00T queries), against ~18 seconds for a real robot to run the same ~360 steps at 20 Hz. Each iteration's 48 episodes take ~29 minutes on four parallel simulators, about two-thirds of every ~45-minute iteration on a single A10G (24 GB VRAM). After living with that loop, I appreciate the appeal of off-policy RL a lot more.
    Next Steps
    • More, and rotating, kitchens. Four fixed training kitchens already lift 30 held-out kitchens from 48.9% to 60.0%, so broader training should transfer further. More groups (one per kitchen) widen each update but, like larger groups, grow collection time linearly. Rotating groups keeps the cost fixed: draw four new kitchens every iteration, so a 10-iteration run trains on 40 kitchens instead of 4. Fresh kitchens are also rarely mastered, so their groups stay mixed instead of coming back all-success.
    • More epochs per iteration (an off-policy extension). An extra training epoch costs ~4 minutes, against ~29 minutes to collect fresh rollouts, so reusing each batch longer is cheap. Jitter makes this safe: $\xi$ is redrawn on every forward pass, so each epoch sees new perturbations and success rows keep a fresh learning signal, while failure rows that use up their erosion budget are simply clipped.
    • Dynamic jitter. Both experiments needed a hand-tuned increase in λ when learning stalled (0.125 → 0.25 for the single state, 0.25 → 0.4 for four kitchens). A controller could raise λ when success rows run out of headroom and lower it once the field has absorbed it, possibly varying λ across the extra epochs above.
    • Velocity anchor. Penalize how far the trained velocity field drifts from base GR00T's at the same inputs, $\|v_\theta - v_{\text{base}}\|^2$. This pulls every output direction back toward the pretrained field, protecting the skills GR00T already has over longer runs (similar to a KL penalty).
    • Pre-close chunk filtering. Within a group, successes and failures approach the mug almost identically, so their approach chunks get opposite advantages and largely cancel. Keeping only a failure's last few chunks before the gripper closes would remove that cancellation: the successes' approach is reinforced cleanly, and the failure signal concentrates on the failed grasp. It complements the post-reopen truncation, which trims what comes after a failed grasp.
